Your heart is pounding. Your chest is tight. You can't catch your breath. Your hands are tingling. You're convinced something is seriously wrong — a heart attack, maybe. Or that you're about to lose control completely.

Then, after ten to twenty minutes, it passes. Nothing happened. You're fine. But you're exhausted, shaken, and terrified it will happen again.

That's a panic attack.

Panic attacks are one of the most common mental health experiences in India — and one of the most misunderstood. They're often dismissed ("just calm down"), misdiagnosed as heart problems (leading to expensive cardiology workups), or simply endured in silence because people don't know what they're dealing with.

This guide explains what panic attacks actually are, why they happen, and — most importantly — what actually stops them.

## What Is a Panic Attack?

A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear or discomfort that peaks within minutes and includes at least four of these symptoms:

- Racing or pounding heart (palpitations)
- Sweating
- Trembling or shaking
- Shortness of breath or feeling smothered
- Feeling of choking
- Chest pain or discomfort
- Nausea or stomach distress
- Dizziness, lightheadedness, or feeling faint
- Chills or hot flushes
- Numbness or tingling sensations
- Feelings of unreality (derealisation) or being detached from yourself (depersonalisation)
- Fear of losing control or "going crazy"
- Fear of dying

Panic attacks can occur in the context of an anxiety disorder (panic disorder, social anxiety, PTSD), or they can happen in people who have no diagnosed condition at all — often triggered by stress, sleep deprivation, caffeine, or nothing identifiable at all.

The critical thing to understand: **panic attacks are not dangerous.** They feel catastrophic. They feel like the worst thing that could happen is happening. They are not. Your body is having a false alarm.

## Why Panic Attacks Happen: The False Alarm System

Your brain contains a threat-detection circuit centred in the amygdala. When it detects danger, it triggers the fight-or-flight response: adrenaline floods your system, your heart rate increases, breathing accelerates, and blood is routed away from your digestive system toward your muscles.

This is useful when the danger is real — a car swerving toward you, a dog charging.

In a panic attack, the alarm fires without a real threat. The body's response is identical to real danger, but there is no danger. The terrifying symptoms — the pounding heart, the breathlessness, the tingling — are your body preparing to fight or flee something that isn't there.

Here's what makes it worse: the symptoms themselves become frightening. You feel your heart pounding. You think "something is wrong with my heart." That thought triggers more fear. More fear triggers more adrenaline. More adrenaline makes your heart pound harder. This is the panic spiral.

**Breaking the spiral** — rather than escaping the situation — is the key to stopping a panic attack.

## 5 Techniques That Actually Stop a Panic Attack

### 1. Controlled Breathing (Physiological Sigh)

Hyperventilation — fast, shallow breathing — drives many of the worst panic symptoms. It drops your CO₂ levels, which causes the tingling, dizziness, and feeling of unreality.

The most effective breathing technique for panic is the **physiological sigh**: a double inhale through the nose (sniff, then top up), followed by a long, slow exhale through the mouth.

Do this 2–3 times. It rapidly deflates the overinflated air sacs in your lungs and resets your CO₂ balance. It is physiologically the fastest way to calm your nervous system — faster than standard box breathing.

After the physiological sigh, settle into a 4-7-8 pattern: inhale for 4 counts, hold for 7, exhale for 8. The extended exhale activates the parasympathetic nervous system.

### 2. The 5-4-3-2-1 Grounding Technique

Panic sends your mind into catastrophic future thinking. Grounding pulls it back to the present.

Name:
- **5 things you can see** (the ceiling fan, a cup, your hands)
- **4 things you can physically feel** (your feet on the floor, fabric on your skin)
- **3 things you can hear** (traffic outside, your own breathing)
- **2 things you can smell**
- **1 thing you can taste**

This technique works by activating your sensory cortex, which competes with the threat-detection circuitry for attention. It doesn't stop the physiology immediately, but it keeps you anchored in reality during the worst of the wave.

### 3. Paradoxical Intention (Don't Fight It)

This is counterintuitive but highly effective: **instead of trying to stop the panic, try to make it worse.**

Tell yourself: "Go ahead. Have the heart attack you're worried about. Faint. Go crazy. Go ahead."

This sounds absurd — but the resistance and fighting-against is what maintains the spiral. When you stop fighting and lean into the sensations, you interrupt the feedback loop. The adrenaline has nowhere to go. It burns off.

This is a core technique in CBT for panic disorder, and it works precisely because it eliminates the fear-of-fear that sustains the attack.

### 4. Cold Water on the Face

Splash cold water on your face, or briefly submerge your face in cold water. This triggers the **diving reflex** — a rapid, involuntary decrease in heart rate. It's one of the fastest physiological hacks for acute panic.

If you're somewhere cold water isn't accessible, hold ice cubes or press something cold against your wrists or the back of your neck.

### 5. Verbal Self-Narration

Say out loud (or in your head, if in public): "This is a panic attack. I am not dying. My body is having a false alarm. This will pass in 10–15 minutes. I am safe."

Research on affect labelling shows that naming an emotion reduces its intensity by activating the prefrontal cortex, which has inhibitory connections to the amygdala. Narrating what is happening to yourself — calmly, accurately — recruits the rational brain while the emotional brain is in overdrive.

## What Not to Do During a Panic Attack

**Don't leave the situation immediately.** Avoidance teaches your brain that the situation was dangerous. Next time, it will trigger panic sooner. Staying through the discomfort (while using the techniques above) is the long-term path to fewer panic attacks.

**Don't breathe into a paper bag.** This is outdated advice. In some people it makes symptoms worse.

**Don't tell yourself to "just calm down."** Instruction without technique achieves nothing.

**Don't catastrophise the catastrophe.** "I'm panicking again and that means I'll never get better." This thought is not true and adds a second layer of suffering to the first.

## Panic Disorder: When to Seek Help

A single panic attack is not a disorder. But if you:
- Have recurrent, unexpected panic attacks
- Spend significant time worrying about the next attack
- Change your behaviour to avoid situations where you might panic (stopped using public transport, crowded spaces, going too far from home)

...you may have panic disorder, which affects an estimated 2–3% of the population and is highly treatable.

**CBT (Cognitive Behavioural Therapy) with interoceptive exposure** is the gold-standard treatment — significantly more effective than medication alone for long-term outcomes. A psychologist trained in anxiety disorders can get most people with panic disorder to significant improvement in 8–12 sessions.

## Understanding Your Triggers

Most panic attacks don't come from nowhere, even when they feel like they do. Common triggers:

- **Sleep deprivation** — drastically lowers the threshold for amygdala activation
- **Caffeine** — directly increases adrenaline, mimics early panic physiology
- **Dehydration** — increases heart rate and dizziness
- **Chronic stress accumulation** — the body's allostatic load eventually overflows
- **Specific situations** — crowds, driving, enclosed spaces, conflict
- **Bodily sensations** — in panic disorder, noticing your own heartbeat can trigger a spiral

Tracking your emotional state daily — not just during attacks, but between them — helps you identify patterns. What did you feel in the 24 hours before an attack? What was your sleep quality? This data is often more revealing than the attack itself.

## The Long Game

Panic attacks are your nervous system's way of saying: *something is under too much pressure.* The techniques above help you survive the moment. But reducing their frequency long-term means addressing the underlying load.

That means: sleep, movement, connection, and — for many people — learning to process the emotions that are building up between the visible moments of crisis.
