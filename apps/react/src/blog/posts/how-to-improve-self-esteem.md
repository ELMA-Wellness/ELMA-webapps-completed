Self-esteem is not confidence. Confidence is situation-specific — you can be confident at work and paralysed in relationships. Self-esteem is deeper: it's your underlying sense of your own worth.

Low self-esteem is not a personality type or a character flaw. It is a learned pattern — usually formed in response to early experiences of criticism, rejection, or neglect — that has become a default way of interpreting yourself and the world.

And like any learned pattern, it can be changed.

Not quickly. Not by repeating affirmations in the mirror. But genuinely, durably, if you work at it with the right approaches.

Here are eight that are backed by research.

## 1. Understand the Difference Between Self-Esteem and Self-Worth

Before doing anything else, it helps to understand what self-esteem actually is — because most people have been pursuing it in ways that make it worse.

**Self-esteem based on performance** ("I feel good about myself when I succeed, bad when I fail") is inherently unstable. It rises and falls with outcomes you don't fully control. Research by Jennifer Crocker at Ohio State found that people who base self-worth on performance show higher levels of stress, anxiety, and depression — not lower.

**Self-esteem based on self-worth** is different. It means believing that you have value as a person independent of your achievements, appearance, or what others think of you. This is stable because it isn't contingent on external outcomes.

The goal is not to chase good feelings about yourself. The goal is to stop treating your intrinsic value as a question that needs ongoing evidence.

## 2. Challenge the Inner Critic With Evidence

Low self-esteem is maintained by a voice inside that comments negatively on you constantly. "You're not smart enough." "They'll realise you don't belong here." "Why would anyone want to be with you."

CBT calls this **cognitive distortion** — thinking that feels true but doesn't match reality.

The most effective technique for this is **cognitive restructuring**: when a self-critical thought appears, treat it like a hypothesis rather than a fact. Ask:

- What is the actual evidence for this thought?
- What is the evidence against it?
- What would I say to a close friend who had this thought about themselves?
- What is a more accurate, balanced way to see this situation?

This doesn't mean forced positivity. It means accuracy. The inner critic is usually not accurate — it takes the worst interpretation of every situation and presents it as fact.

## 3. Stop Comparing Yourself to Social Media Versions of Other People

Social comparison is a core engine of low self-esteem. It is also, in the social media era, almost unavoidably constant.

The problem: you are comparing your interior experience (your doubts, failures, insecurities) to other people's curated exteriors. You know exactly what's behind your own highlight reel. You have no idea what's behind theirs.

Research consistently shows that social media use is correlated with lower self-esteem, particularly in young adults — not because the people on social media are actually doing better, but because the comparison is structurally unfair.

Practical steps:
- Reduce time on platforms that trigger the most comparison
- Actively follow accounts that show reality, not curation
- When you notice comparison happening, name it: "I'm comparing my inside to their outside"
- Practice comparing yourself to your past self rather than to other people

## 4. Act In Line With Your Values

One of the most reliable sources of genuine self-respect is living according to what you actually believe in.

When you behave in ways that contradict your values — being dishonest, backing down when you shouldn't, treating people worse than you think they deserve — your self-esteem drops. Not because you've "failed" by some external standard, but because you've failed yourself.

Conversely, when you act with integrity — saying the harder truth, showing up when it costs you something, treating people well even when no one is watching — your self-respect grows.

This is not about being perfect. It's about noticing the gap between your values and your behaviour, and narrowing it where possible.

## 5. Build Competence Deliberately

Competence is not the same as self-esteem — but it builds it.

When you do hard things and succeed, even small hard things, it provides your brain with genuine evidence that you are capable. This evidence accumulates. It becomes a story you can tell about yourself that isn't just theory.

The key is **deliberate challenge in domains that matter to you**. Not challenge imposed from outside. Not achievement in areas you've been told to value. Things you actually care about.

This is why it's worth identifying what you're genuinely interested in and pursuing depth there, rather than pursuing whatever brings the most social approval.

## 6. Practise Self-Compassion Instead of Self-Criticism

This is counterintuitive for many people. If I'm too easy on myself, won't I become lazy or complacent?

The research answer is clear: no.

Studies by Kristin Neff, who developed the field of self-compassion research, consistently show that self-compassionate people:
- Take more personal responsibility for their mistakes (not less)
- Are more motivated to improve after failure (not less)
- Have significantly better mental health outcomes

Self-compassion means treating yourself the way you would treat a good friend who had made the same mistake. Not excusing the mistake. Not pretending it didn't matter. But responding with care, understanding, and proportionality — rather than relentless criticism.

Three components of self-compassion (Neff):
1. **Self-kindness** — treating yourself with warmth rather than harsh judgement
2. **Common humanity** — recognising that suffering and failure are part of the shared human experience, not evidence of your unique deficiency
3. **Mindfulness** — holding painful feelings with awareness rather than suppressing or over-identifying with them

## 7. Choose Your Relationships Carefully

The people around you have a profound effect on your self-esteem — either confirming your inner critic or contradicting it.

People with low self-esteem often attract and tolerate relationships that reinforce negative self-beliefs. A critical partner confirms "I'm not good enough." A dismissive friend confirms "my needs don't matter."

Gradually — not overnight, not by cutting people off impulsively — move toward relationships where you are treated with consistent respect and warmth. Limit time with people who routinely make you feel diminished.

This is not about surrounding yourself with flattery. It's about spending time with people whose behaviour gives you accurate, positive evidence about your value.

## 8. Track Your Emotional Patterns Over Time

Low self-esteem is often invisible in the moment — it operates as a background assumption rather than a conscious thought. You don't think "I'm worthless." You just find yourself avoiding opportunities, undervaluing your contributions, or feeling inexplicably bad after certain interactions.

Tracking your emotional state over time — not just when you're in crisis, but daily — makes these patterns visible. You start to see: *every time I'm around this person, I feel smaller. Every time I accomplish something I care about, I feel my actual self again.* 

Visible patterns can be worked with. Invisible patterns just run.

## A Note on Serious Low Self-Esteem

If your low self-esteem is severe — if it involves persistent thoughts that you don't deserve to exist, that others would be better off without you, or that you are fundamentally broken — please seek professional support.

These are not philosophical positions. They are symptoms of a psychological state that responds well to treatment. A psychologist can help you work through the experiences and thought patterns that created them.
